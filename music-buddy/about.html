# Music Buddy

## Executive summary

Music Buddy is Dave's personal music companion: the keeper of his taste, the builder of his playlists, his guide into classical music, and his coach when he picks up an instrument. It starts from his real rotation — reggae-rock/dub (Slightly Stoopid, Fortunate Youth, Stick Figure) and a taste that goes all over the map — and grows with him toward three goals: listen more, know classical music better, and ultimately learn to play. Less algorithm, more trusted friend who knows your ears.

## The idea

Dave wants music to be a bigger part of his life, and he wants it tracked the way the buddy system tracks everything else: as a living slice of life with memory. Music Buddy holds his taste profile (what he loves, what he's exploring), builds and maintains custom playlists, feeds him discovery in the lanes he likes, designs a classical-music on-ramp for a curious adult beginner, and coaches his instrument journey — guitars at home, a keyboard in the family. One buddy for the whole musical life, because the listening feeds the learning: what you love is what you'll want to play.

## How it works

Taste profile: seeded from his real rotation — Slightly Stoopid, Fortunate Youth, Stick Figure (reggae-rock/dub) — and refined over time from what Dave plays, skips, and loves. (Corrected 2026-10-12: an early draft centered Saint Germain; Dave confirmed that was Apple-generated background music, not his taste.) Playlists: custom-built for moods and moments, evolving with his taste. # Bob Dylan — A Primer on the Message
*Built for Dave, 2026-10-12. You're not crazy about the music — this is about the words, and why Steve Jobs cared so much.*

## The 30-second version

Bob Dylan took the American folk song — three chords and a story — and turned it into literature. He wrote the defining protest anthems of the 1960s, then walked away from them at the height of his fame to reinvent himself, repeatedly, for sixty years. In 2016 he became the first musician ever awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition." His core message, across every era: **don't stand still, don't accept the official story, keep moving.** "If you are not busy being born, you are busy dying."

## Dylan and Steve Jobs — the thread you asked about

Jobs wasn't a casual fan; Dylan was his hero, and he said so on the record for decades:

- **1984, Macintosh unveiling (Jan 24):** Jobs opened the most important presentation of his life by quoting Dylan — the second verse of "The Times They Are A-Changin'," addressed to the writers and critics in the room. He was telling the industry: the old order is finished.
- **1985, Playboy interview:** "Woz and I very much liked Bob Dylan's poetry, and we spent a lot of time thinking about a lot of that stuff."
- **1998:** "One of my role models is Bob Dylan... Dylan and Picasso were always risking failure." He admired that Dylan could have sung protest songs forever and gotten rich, but walked away instead — going electric in 1965, alienating his entire audience. "His 1966 Europe tour was his greatest."
- **2004:** Jobs finally met Dylan near Palo Alto — two hours on a patio. Jobs told his biographer he was nervous, afraid Dylan would be a caricature of himself. "He was as sharp as a tack. He was everything I'd hoped." Dylan told him the songs "just came through me, it wasn't like I was having to compose them."
- **2006:** Jobs personally courted Dylan into an iPod commercial and a $199 digital boxed set of everything Dylan had ever recorded — the first time Dylan licensed his catalog that way. Jobs was so exacting he made Dylan retape the entire ad. It put Dylan back at the top of the charts for the first time since 1976.
- **2007, iPhone unveiling:** Jobs demoed the iPhone's music playback with "Like a Rolling Stone."
- His iPod reportedly held 21 Dylan albums, including all six volumes of the bootleg series. Asked to choose between Dylan/Beatles and the Stones if the vault were on fire: "Nobody could have been Dylan or the Beatles."

What Jobs took from Dylan: **reinvention as a discipline, authenticity as a strategy, and the willingness to alienate your audience to stay an artist.** That's the operating system underneath Apple.

## The message in five acts

**Act 1 — The protest bard (1962–64).** A 21-year-old writes the conscience of a generation: war, civil rights, ordinary people crushed by power. The message: *look at what's actually happening, and say it plainly.* This is the Dylan most people mean when they say "Dylan."

**Act 2 — The betrayal (1965–66).** At the peak of folk sainthood, he plugs in an electric guitar. The folk crowd calls him Judas — literally, shouted at him on stage in Manchester in 1966. He plays louder. The message: *never let your audience decide who you are.* This is the act Jobs loved most.

**Act 3 — The recluse (1967–70s).** After a motorcycle crash, he vanishes to Woodstock, records strange homespun tapes in a basement, then makes quiet country records. The message: *fame is a trap; the work is the thing.*

**Act 4 — The wanderer (1975–90s).** Divorce record (*Blood on the Tracks*), born-again Christian records, endless touring — the "Never Ending Tour" that ran for decades. The message: *keep moving; identity is a verb.*

**Act 5 — The elder (1997–present).** Written off, then *Time Out of Mind* wins Album of the Year. *Modern Times* at 65. The Nobel at 75 — which he barely acknowledged, skipped the ceremony, then delivered a 26-minute lecture about *Moby-Dick*, *All Quiet on the Western Front*, and *The Odyssey* as the foundations of his songwriting. The message, from his lecture: *"If a song moves you, that's all that's important."* Songs are meant to be sung, not read — he almost talked the Nobel committee out of their own prize.

## Five songs to read first (lyrics-first — that's your entry point)

1. **"The Times They Are A-Changin'" (1964)** — the one Jobs quoted. A warning to everyone in power: the wheel is still in spin. Read it as a memo to any incumbent.
2. **"It's Alright, Ma (I'm Only Bleeding)" (1965)** — his densest lyric; a furious X-ray of a phony culture. Source of "busy being born / busy dying."
3. **"Like a Rolling Stone" (1965)** — six minutes of pure venom and liberation, aimed at someone who had everything and lost it. The song Jobs played to demo the iPhone.
4. **"Shelter from the Storm" (1975)** — the tender one; Jobs listed it among his favorites. About finding refuge in another person when the world goes cold.
5. **"Not Dark Yet" (1997)** — late Dylan on mortality, written at 56: every line feels carved. The elder looking back without flinching.

## On your stereo

Dylan's best-sounding records reward a good system: *Highway 61 Revisited* (1965) — the electric betrayal, all snarl and organ; *Blood on the Tracks* (1975) — intimate, every guitar string audible; *Desire* (1976) — Jobs's own copy, violin all over it, huge soundstage. If you only play one side: side one of *Highway 61*.

## For our session — things to chew on

- Jobs admired Dylan's *reinvention*, not his melodies. Where in your own work have you had a "go electric" moment — or avoided one?
- Dylan's Nobel lecture claims songs aren't literature, they're meant to be sung. But you're coming at him through the words. Does the message survive without the music?
- "If you are not busy being born, you are busy dying" — where does that land for you right now?
