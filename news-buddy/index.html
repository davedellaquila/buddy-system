Systems & security

Engineering

What the FAA ground stop teaches about shared dependencies: redundancy fails when the backup shares the primary’s failure causes — failure domains must be small and separated

DEV Community (Control Plane), Thursday: an engineering post-mortem of the Nov. 13 ground stop — excess traffic from El Dorado, Arkansas and Amarillo, Texas destabilized 17 of 22 en route centers for about 4 hours 40 minutes — organized around one lesson: redundancy helps only when the backup does not share the primary’s failure causes. The software equivalents are named plainly: retry storms, connection floods, resource exhaustion, correlated failures in systems that look redundant but share a dependency. Root cause remains officially under investigation (Duffy, Nov. 17: ’we don’t have clarity yet’); HPE maintains the routers worked properly; the FBI is examining a unit. The analysis is a vendor’s, and labeled as such in its own disclosure — the failure-domain argument stands on the published sequence.

DEV Community

The FAA leases its network, and the contract sets the design: the leased-network analysis counts at least 34 FAA telecom failures since 1988 and lists what the AT&T successor contract must require

Mike Hammett’s network-outage series, this week: a structural account of Nov. 13 — instability from about 3:30 p.m. ET, a router at an L3Harris copper-to-fiber cutover in El Dorado flooding the system with over 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, a broadcast feature since disabled across the Juniper fleet — arguing the root condition is contractual: the FAA leases the network, and the lease’s design terms decide what resilience exists. The prescription is specific: diversity audits, staged changes, out-of-band management, and a third non-wireline path written into the new AT&T contract, required of L3Harris until the replacement lands. Duffy’s $50 billion modernization ask, and a late-2028/early-2029 replacement if funded, frame the stakes.
