America’s debt soars as Washington’s fiscal hawks vanish: public debt hits 100% of GDP, interest now exceeds military spending — and the midterms barely mention it

The Wall Street Journal

The Journal’s Oct. 31 10-Point lead (Rubin and Luhnow): something is missing from this year’s midterm campaigns — the debt. In 2018, 140 political ads mentioned the debt or deficit; this year, just 30 have. Federal debt held by the public has reached 100% of GDP for the first time in 80 years. The same edition: OpenAI has raised less than half of the $30 billion it targeted; ACA premiums will rise 13% in 2027; and Christine Lagarde wagers her brand of politics still has a place.

Why it matters: The Journal's lead reframes the midterms: debt at 100% of GDP and interest above defense spending, campaigned on by almost no one (30 ads vs 140 in 2018). The silence is the story.

Republican anxiety mounts in the final days: the Journal’s last pre-election poll shows 60% of voters want Congress to be a check on Trump

The Wall Street Journal

The Journal, Oct. 30-31: with disquieting signals of widespread midterm losses, Republicans are scrambling to play defense even in seats previously seen as strongholds — a rural New York district Trump carried by 21 points now polls 4 points against the GOP candidate. The final Journal poll (1,500 voters, Oct. 14-22) finds Democrats ahead by 7 on the generic ballot, Trump’s approval at 40% against 59% disapproval — the highest dissatisfaction for any president heading into a midterm in Journal polling since 1990 — and Democrats now rated the better economic managers, a 15-point swing since July 2025.

Why it matters: The final Journal poll is the GOP's warning label: 59% disapproval, a 7-point generic deficit, and the economy rating flipped to the Democrats. Kansas and Iowa on Sunday are defense, not offense.

How Iran battered U.S. bases and caused billions in damage: 119 buildings hit at 13 installations, reconstruction alone priced above $4 billion

The Wall Street Journal

The Journal’s investigation, from European Space Agency satellite imagery, Iranian military statements and U.S. officials: Iran’s July shift — aiming directly at the buildings where troops sleep — killed three servicemembers at Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base on July 17 and damaged at least 19 buildings there. More than 20 U.S. servicemembers have died in war-related activity since Feb. 28. The war’s cost estimate stands at $47.1 billion as of Oct. 16, excluding base damage the Pentagon prices at $5-15 billion. CSIS estimates reconstruction alone — without the equipment inside — at more than $4 billion.

Why it matters: The war's ledger, measured from orbit: $47.1B spent, $5-15B of base damage, and a tactics shift aimed at where troops sleep. Read it before any 'what next in Iran' argument.

Binance’s $6.3 billion hack is just the start of a risky new era

The New York Times

Also on the Times’ Oct. 31 front: the paper’s business desk frames the Binance theft as a structural story, not a one-off — the largest exchange, embedded in mainstream finance, drained through an engineer’s laptop while stablecoin issuers froze what they could and the bitcoin could not be frozen at all.

Why it matters: The Times agrees with the BBC: the heist is the era, not the incident. When the largest exchange is drained through a laptop, 'crypto risk' stops being a niche phrase.

The polls have been unusually similar: in 13 of 27 races, surveys vary less than they should — pollsters are correcting their samples the same way

The Washington Post

The Post’s polling analysis, via Political Wire: across 27 races the paper examined — including the Senate races in Maine and Texas — 13 show less variation among polls than would normally be expected. One possible reason: as low-propensity voters get harder to reach, many pollsters now weight by recalled 2024 vote, which can make polls look alike even when they reached different people. Three days out, the sameness itself is the uncertainty.

Why it matters: A methods story with real stakes: if pollsters all correct the same way, the polls can be uniformly wrong together. Tuesday's results will grade the correction, not just the candidates.
