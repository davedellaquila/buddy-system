The Front Pages

What Sunday's papers are leading with: the Journal fronts OpenAI's halved funding round ($11B of $30B, at $1.25T) and investigates the death of an Nvidia prodigy; the Times leads with an analysis of Trump's speeches at 80 and fronts Michigan's Senate brawl; the Post reports the Social Security chief's campaign email and the second dismissal of Trump's $10B Journal suit. OpenAI raises less than half of its $30 billion target: about $11 billion secured at a $1.25 trillion valuation, the target now cut to $20 billion

The Wall Street Journal

The Journal’s front: after tepid interest from some large investors, OpenAI has secured roughly $11 billion of the $30 billion it targeted, at a $1.25 trillion valuation — below the $1.4 trillion it had hoped for — and now hopes to raise up to $20 billion, turning to family offices and wealthy individuals. The hiccup lands amid a brutal tech selloff and as Anthropic’s IPO paperwork shows its revenue more than doubling quarter to quarter to $11.6 billion against OpenAI’s $6.7 billion (up about 18%), with Anthropic at a small operating profit and OpenAI’s losses deepening. OpenAI is preparing to go public as soon as early next year.

Why it matters: The funding market is grading the AI trade's biggest name like a stock: half the target raised, valuation trimmed $150B, Anthropic's IPO competing for the same dollars -- and printing better growth numbers.

The sudden death of an Nvidia AI prodigy leaves behind bitter accusations

The Wall Street Journal

The Journal’s investigation, Palo Alto: Irfan Al-Hussaini, a Georgia Tech PhD and Nvidia senior data scientist, died at Stanford Medical Center on July 30 at 32, eight days after his wife Fabia Farlin Athena — a Stanford researcher and Forbes 30 Under 30 honoree for work on AI energy use — called 911. The medical examiner ruled suicide; his family is publicly challenging the ruling, Bangladesh’s consul general has asked Palo Alto police for a comprehensive investigation, and the family’s attorney says questions remain. Police say there is still no evidence of foul play; the autopsy found no reported evidence of a physical struggle. Vigils have been held at four universities.

Why it matters: A hard, careful read: a young researcher's death, a contested ruling, and a family's public campaign. The Journal reports what is known and labels what is not -- the discipline is part of the story.

Here’s where affordable housing is on the ballot this cycle: six states, billions in bonds, and property-tax fights

The Wall Street Journal

From the Journal’s midterm live coverage: at least six states and several municipalities have housing measures on Tuesday’s ballot, as affordability tops voter concerns. California’s measure would authorize billions for state housing programs plus $1.25 billion for a veterans home-loan program; Rhode Island votes on $120 million in bonds for affordable housing; Wyoming’s would exempt 50% of a primary residence’s value from property tax. The Journal also sat seven midterm candidates down to give their fixes for the economy — tariffs drew rebukes from the right and the left alike.

Why it matters: California's own measure is in here: billions for housing programs plus $1.25B for veterans' home loans. Affordability is the ballot's through-line in at least six states.

An analysis of Trump’s speeches at age 80: more long-winded, more profane, more absolutist — the Times’ Sunday lead

The New York Times

The Times leads its Sunday front with a linguistic and behavioral assessment of the president’s unscripted rally remarks, per the front-page roundups: ’Mr. Trump, who last year became the oldest person ever inaugurated as president and turned 80 in June, has demonstrated physical stamina as he barnstorms the country in advance of midterm elections. But his performances on the road and at the White House this fall have produced many head-scratching moments that have revived questions about his age and stability.’ The paper finds him ’more long-winded, more profane and more absolutist than when he ran for president a decade ago.’ Dave’s NYT subscription reads the full piece on his own devices.

Why it matters: The Times' Sunday lead is a style-as-substance audit: stamina conceded, coherence questioned. Whatever your politics, the paper of record putting age and stability on A1 two days before the vote is the signal.

Michigan’s Senate race is ’one of the nastiest of the year’ — and it could decide control of the chamber

The New York Times

Also on the Times’ front, per Political Wire’s quotes: the Michigan race between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers ’has exposed electoral fault lines over religion, race, inflation, tariffs and, of course, President Trump,’ has become ’the year’s most consequential contest for Democrats as they look ahead to 2028,’ and tests ’moderate and independent voters’ appetite for a combative brand of unalloyed progressivism’ after El-Sayed’s primary upset of the establishment choice. Trump spent Saturday attacking El-Sayed personally at Mar-a-Lago.

Why it matters: The Senate may turn on Michigan: a progressive insurgent, a faith fight Trump keeps inflaming, and a chamber margin thin enough that one nasty race decides it.

’That previous source of political strength now threatens to topple Republicans’: cost-of-living fury as the campaign’s closing force

The New York Times

The Times’ campaign analysis on the front: ’The fate of the party’s control of Congress may well hinge on the depth of voters’ fury with the rising cost of living — and the president’s unmet promises to do something about it.’ Republican concern is ’evident as Mr. Trump crisscrosses the country in an 11th-hour campaign sprint,’ presenting the economy as strong while ’inflation has clearly worsened under his party’s stewardship.’ The same file: at least nine of 15 cabinet secretaries have been formally accused of Hatch Act violations for campaigning on official time, alarming ethics experts.

Why it matters: The cost-of-living thread ties the whole front together -- and the Hatch Act count (nine cabinet secretaries accused) says the campaign now runs through the government's own machinery.

The Washington Post

The suit had sought $10 billion. The BBC carries the dismissal on its own front this morning as well.

Shops looted as German cost-of-living protests turn violent

BBC News

The BBC’s lead this morning: protests in cities across Germany — Berlin, Hamburg and Frankfurt among them — turned violent with shops looted, on the same day hundreds of thousands also protested in France. Cost of living is the common cause on both sides of the Rhine, the European echo of the affordability politics dominating the U.S. midterms.

Why it matters: Europe's affordability politics turning physical: looted shops in three German cities while France marches. The same cost-of-living pressure, a different register than America's ballot fights.

Asylum seekers still being sent to France under ’one in, one out’ weeks after the scheme ended

The Guardian

The Guardian’s lead investigation: the Home Office is still putting asylum seekers on planes to France weeks after the controversial ’one in, one out’ scheme ended on Oct. 1 — a chartered flight carried a group on Wednesday, dozens remain detained, and another flight is scheduled for Nov. 17. Freedom-of-information data shows seven of the scheme’s 23 removal flights were cancelled between July and September. At least seven people sent to France have since returned to Britain.

Why it matters: A scheme that ended on paper but not at the airport: the Guardian's detention and flight details are the accountability gap in one investigation. Another removal flight is already booked for Nov. 17.

Nikkei plunges as the AI selloff overwhelms the Bank of Japan’s rate hold: -2.12% to 63,290.11, chip equipment down 9-15%

Nikkei Asia

Nikkei Asia’s lead, via the Reuters-based wrap: Friday’s Tokyo session followed Wall Street’s AI rout — Advantest -9.1%, Tokyo Electron -5.6%, SoftBank -6.7%, Ibiden -15.2%, Lasertec -13.8% — while the broader Topix fell a milder 1.3%, the selling concentrated in the AI supply chain. The Nikkei still finished the week +0.2%. Tokyo core-core inflation slowed to 2.6% in October from 2.9%.

Why it matters: Tokyo's tape says the AI verdict travels the supply chain: the testers and substrate makers fell 9-15% while the broad market fell 1.3%. Exposure concentration is the risk map.

The BoJ holds at 1.25% in an 8-1 vote — and accelerates its ETF sales; December is priced at ~80% for a hike

Nikkei Asia

Nikkei Asia’s second story: the Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at 1.25% on Friday, 8-1, with board member Hajime Takata dissenting for 1.5%. The bank acknowledged underlying inflation near 2% and voted to accelerate sales of its ETF holdings — annual sales rising from about 330 billion yen to about 860 billion yen at book value from January 2027. ANZ expects 25bp hikes in December and again in March; the 10-year JGB yield eased toward 2.78% as bonds drew buyers.

Why it matters: The BoJ is quietly becoming the world's tightening outlier -- 8-1 to hold, an ETF-sale acceleration, December priced at 80%. Two central banks (Tokyo, Dec. 9 Fed) now share one calendar.
